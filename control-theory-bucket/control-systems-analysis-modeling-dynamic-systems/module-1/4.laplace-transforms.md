# Laplace Transform Review

$$F(s) = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} f(t)e^{-st} dt $$

- t represents time
- s is a complex frequency
- F(s) is the frequency domain representation of the time domain signal f(t)

---

**Step Function:**

$f(t) = a \; 1 (t) \quad where \quad 1(t) = \begin{cases}
1, & t \ge 0 \\
0, & t < 0
\end{cases}$

**What are Laplace Transforms?**
Laplace transforms are especially useful in analyzing linear time-invariant systems. 

The Laplace transform of a signal $f(t)$ is defined as:
$$F(s) = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} f(t) e^{-st} dt$$

* $t$ represents time
* $s$ is a complex frequency
* $F(s)$ is the frequency domain representation of the time domain signal $f(t)$

**Example: Laplace Transform of a Scaled Step Function**
Consider $f(t) = a \times 1(t)$, where:
* $a$ is a constant gain
* $1(t)$ is the unit step function, defined as $1$ when $t \geq 0$ and $0$ otherwise

To compute the Laplace transform:
$$F(s) = \int_0^\infty a \times e^{-st} dt$$

Evaluating the integral:
$$F(s) = \frac{a}{s}$$

*Note: The real part of $s$ must be greater than 0 for the integral to converge.*

**Region of Convergence**
The region of convergence (ROC) is important because without it, a Laplace transform can correspond to more than one time-domain signal.

* For causal signals (non-zero only for $t \geq 0$), the ROC is typically $\text{Re}(s) > \text{some real number}$.
* In this course, we generally assume causal signals and often omit specifying the ROC.

**Laplace Transform Table (Assuming Causal Signals)**

| Function $f(t)$ | Laplace Transform $F(s)$ |
| :--- | :--- |
| $1(t)$ | $\frac{1}{s}$ |
| $t \times 1(t)$ | $\frac{1}{s^2}$ |
| $t^2 \times 1(t)$ | $\frac{2}{s^3}$ |
| $e^{-at} \times 1(t)$ | $\frac{1}{s+a}$ |

**One-Sided Laplace Transform**
Since we assume causal signals, we use the one-sided Laplace transform:
$$F(s) = \int_{0^-}^\infty f(t) e^{-st} dt$$

$0^-$ allows inclusion of impulsive inputs at $t=0$.

**Example: Laplace Transform of $\cos(at)$**
Assuming the signal is causal:
$$F(s) = \int_0^\infty \cos(at) e^{-st} dt$$

To compute this integral, use Euler's formula:
$$\cos x = \frac{e^{jx} + e^{-jx}}{2}$$
where $j$ is the imaginary unit.

Substitute $\cos(at)$ with this expression, then integrate the exponentials, which is straightforward.

**Euler's Formula and the Unit Circle**
* The unit circle in the complex plane has radius 1.
* $e^{jx}$ lies on the unit circle with angle $x$.
* Real part of $e^{jx}$ is $\cos x$.
* Imaginary part of $e^{jx}$ is $j \sin x$.
* Adding $e^{jx}$ and $e^{-jx}$ cancels imaginary parts, giving $2 \cos x$.

**Summary**
* Laplace transforms are useful for analyzing signals and linear time-invariant systems.
* The transform converts time-domain signals into frequency-domain representations.
* We reviewed the definition and computation of Laplace transforms for basic functions.
* Euler's formula helps in computing transforms of trigonometric functions like cosine.
