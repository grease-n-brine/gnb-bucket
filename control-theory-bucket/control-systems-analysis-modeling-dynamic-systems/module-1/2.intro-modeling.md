# Introduction to Modeling

## Modeling the plant

- **Analytically derived models**
    - Based on fundamental laws (Newton, Kirchoff, etc.)
    - Used for simpler systems
    - Yields differential equations as function of system parameters
    - More general and gives better insight

- **Experimentally derived models (system identification)**
    - Time-domain, frequency-domain, and other methods
    - Used for more complex systems
    - Yields numerical differential equations or transfer functions for particular system

**1. What makes a system linear?**

**A.** Linear systems obey the superposition principle.

$if, u_1 (t) \longrightarrow y_1 (t) \And u_2 (t) \longrightarrow y_2 (t) $

$then, \alpha u_1 (t) + \beta u_2 (t) \longrightarrow \alpha y_1 (t) + \beta y_2 (t)$


**2. What makes a system time-invariant?**

**A.** A time- invariant system is such that, for any choice of Delay $T$, the response $y(t - T)$ to an input $u(t - T)$ matches the response $y(t)$ to an input $u(t)$

**Note: Most systems are non-linear, but can be closely approximated**

## Continuous-Time vs. Digital Control

In most modern control systems, the plant is continuous-time, but the controller is often implemented on a micro processor which results in digital control.

When sampling speeds are fast, the digital controller can be approximated as a continous-time controller.
