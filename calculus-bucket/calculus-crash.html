Module 1: The Philosophy — Discrete vs. Continuous

As programmers and computer scientists, we spend most of our lives in a discrete world: integers, steps in a for loop, individual pixels on a screen, or physics frames running at fixed intervals.

Real-world physics and continuous systems don't work in discrete jumps—they sweep smoothly through infinite points. Calculus is the mathematical framework built to handle infinity by connecting discrete steps to continuous flow.

"Calculus is the art of taking something continuous, chopping it into infinitely small discrete pieces, and adding them back up to find the total behavior."

Module 2: Core Concepts & The CS Bridge

1. From Discrete Loops to Continuous Accumulation

When you want to find total accumulation in code, you write a loop that sums discrete steps. When time intervals shrink toward zero ($dt \to 0$), that discrete loop becomes an integral.

Step 1: The Discrete Loop

Imagine tracking distance by checking speed every 1 second: $\text{Distance} = \text{velocity} \times \Delta t$. This forms a blocky Riemann sum approximation.

Step 2: Shrinking the Time Step

As your time slice $dt$ shrinks closer to zero, your number of calculation steps approaches infinity, eliminating gaps.

Step 3: The Integral Limit

The sum becomes a definite integral: $\int f(t) dt$, capturing absolute accumulated area under the curve with zero error.

2. Derivatives vs. Integrals

  • Derivatives: Breaking things down to find the instantaneous rate of change right now (e.g., computing gradients to optimize loss functions in machine learning).
  • Integrals: Accumulating rates over time to find total outcomes (e.g., total energy generated, total distance traveled).

Module 3: Step-by-Step Worked Examples

Let's put integration logic into practice with concrete problem types.

Example 1: Evaluating a Definite Integral

Calculate total data download volume where download rate is $f(t) = 3t^2$ over the time interval from $t = 0$ to $t = 2$ seconds.

Step 1: Set up the integral

Write the definite integral with bounds: $\int_{0}^{2} 3t^2 \, dt$.

Step 2: Find the anti-derivative

Using the power rule for integration, the anti-derivative of $3t^2$ is $t^3$ (since $\frac{d}{dt}(t^3) = 3t^2$).

Step 3: Apply boundaries

Evaluate $t^3$ from 0 to 2: $(2^3) - (0^3) = 8 - 0 = \mathbf{8 \text{ MB}}$.

Example 2: Average Value of a Signal

Find the average temperature of a component modeled by $T(t) = 4t$ from $t = 1$ to $t = 3$ minutes.

Step 1: Use the average value formula

Average value = $\frac{1}{b-a} \int_{a}^{b} f(t) \, dt$. Here, $\frac{1}{3-1} = \frac{1}{2}$.

Step 2: Evaluate the integral

Integrate $4t$, yielding anti-derivative $2t^2$ evaluated from 1 to 3.

Step 3: Compute final value

$(2(3^2)) - (2(1^2)) = 18 - 2 = 16$. Multiply by $\frac{1}{2}$ to get an average temperature of $\mathbf{8}$.
